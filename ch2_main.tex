\begin{abstract}
\agentformat{For a Data Descriptor, revise the abstract sentence reporting higher same-source correlations and decreasing variability so it describes the data and reuse without making a claim about a scientific finding.}

Wire-cut striation marks can provide evidence for forensic source
identification, but publicly available datasets and reproducible
quantitative workflows for analyzing these marks are limited. We present
120 three-dimensional topographic scans in \texttt{x3p} format from
aluminum wires cut with five KWS-105 wire cutters. The study includes
four edges, three locations along each tool, and two replicates for
every tool-edge-location combination. We provide scan metadata, manually
extracted profiles, processed signals, pairwise cross-correlation
values, images, and analysis code. The accompanying workflow covers scan
inspection, profile and signal extraction, cross-correlation-based
alignment, pairwise similarity visualization, and variability assessment
across replicates, stagings, and acquisitions. Technical validation
shows higher cross-correlations for same-source comparisons than for
different-source comparisons and decreasing variability as scanning
conditions become more consistent. The dataset and workflow support the
development, evaluation, and reproducible comparison of quantitative
methods for wire-cut evidence.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Background \& Summary}\label{sec-background-summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Background \& Summary}

In forensic analysis, marks left at crime scenes play an important role,
and forensic examiners are particularly interested in identifying their
origin, a process known as the Source Identification Problem in Forensic
Science \citep{lindleyProblemForensicScience1977}. The forensic science
community focuses on two distinct problems: the \textbf{specific source
problem}, in which examiners aim to determine whether a mark was left by
a particular tool, and the \textbf{common source problem}, in which
examiners aim to determine whether two marks were left by the same tool.
Currently, accepted practice for both problems relies on visual
inspection of physical items under a comparison microscope. Examiners
then report their conclusions according to the Theory of Identification
\citep{afteAssociationFirearmTool1998}, developed by the Association of
Firearm and Toolmark Examiners (AFTE), using one of three categories:
\emph{identification}, in which the marks are believed to have been made
by the same source; \emph{elimination}, in which the marks are believed
to have been made by different tools; and \emph{inconclusive}, in which
the similarities or dissimilarities between marks are insufficient to
support either identification or elimination. This process has been
criticized by the National Research Council (NRC)
\citep{nationalresearchcouncilu.s.StrengtheningForensicScience2009a} and
the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology (PCAST)
\citep{presidentscouncilofadvisorsonscienceandtechnologyForensicScienceCriminal2016}
for its lack of objectivity and absence of quantifiable measurements,
such as error rates.

In discussing error rates, Biedermann
\citep{biedermannStrangePersistenceSource2022} differentiates between
internal and external perspectives. The external perspective allows
general statements based on black-box studies that relate examiners'
conclusions to ground truth; however, it does not consider the evidence
in a particular case. Introducing an internal perspective requires a
quantitative evaluation of the similarity of specific evidence and, in
particular, objective algorithmic evaluations.

To train algorithms, we need data from scenarios with known ground
truth. Because data collected at crime scenes are not suitable for this
purpose, we used specific tools to cut wires under controlled conditions
and collected the resulting marks for training. This approach enables
comparisons between ground truth and the classification results produced
by trained algorithms, with similarity quantified using error rates.

\agentreview{Clarify whether the error rates quantify similarity or the performance of the resulting classifications. The current sentence states that similarity itself is quantified using error rates.}

Advances in microscopy have made it possible to move from optical
two-dimensional imaging to digital three-dimensional topographic
scanning, allowing algorithmic assessment of the similarity between
pairs of forensic pattern evidence
\citep{bonfantiVisualisationConfocalMicroscopy2000}. This transition has
already supported practical advances in bullet and cartridge
comparisons, including visual inspection of bullet line scans from
three-dimensional topographic images
\citep{smithLinescanImagingBallistics2002}, the use of features
extracted by fast Fourier transform (FFT) as the basis of an artificial
intelligence system \citep{liBallisticsProjectileImage2006}, an
automated pilot study based on consecutively matching striae
\citep{chuPilotStudyAutomated2010} (a feature also used by some
laboratories in casework), and the incorporation of three-dimensional
imaging into routine casework at the Alabama Department of Forensic
Sciences (ADFS) \citep{mcclarinAddingObjectiveComponent2015}.

When cut wires are found at a crime scene, understanding the
characteristics of marks left by bladed tools becomes essential. When a
blade cuts a wire, it leaves striations on the surface, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig-cut-microscope}. These striations provide a basis for
similarity assessment and source identification. Similar scans have
supported the development of quantitative methods for analyzing firearms
and other toolmark evidence. For example, studies of screwdriver
striations have examined key factors affecting comparisons, including
angles of attack \citep{baikerToolmarkVariabilityQuality2015a},
rotational axes \citep{garciaInfluenceAxialRotation2017}, and cutting
directions \citep{cuellarAlgorithmForensicToolmark2024}. Foundational
research on forensic toolmarks has also emphasized data collection and
analysis, including the collection and distribution of datasets for
bullet and toolmark analysis
\citep{maNISTBulletSignature2004, zhengNISTBallisticsToolmark2016},
numerical methods for improving accuracy and consistency
\citep{chuAutomaticIdentificationBullet2013, vorburgerApplicationsCrosscorrelationFunctions2011},
and techniques for quantifying similarity
\citep{hareAutomaticMatchingBullet2017, juOpenSourceImplementationCMPS2022}.

Criminal convictions have relied on cut-wire evidence and related
testimony. However, no public database supports assessment of (1) the
similarity between striations on wires cut with the same tool or (2)
error rates in source identification. Public wire-cut datasets are
therefore urgently needed.

In this study, we provide a publicly available dataset of wire-cut
scans, develop a systematic pipeline to analyze the data quantitatively,
and introduce numerical measures for similarity assessment beyond visual
inspection. The dataset includes multiple types of files, as summarized
in Table~\ref{tbl-data-overview}.

To ensure full reproducibility, we describe each step in detail,
including how we collected and processed the wire-cut samples in Cutting
wires, extracted profiles from the scanned wire cuts in Extract
profiles, filtered signals from the profiles in Filtered signals, and
aligned signals using the cross-correlation function (CCF) as a
similarity measure in Align signals. Data Records describes where the
dataset is hosted. Technical Validation evaluates the consistency of the
assumptions and results and assesses variability. Usage Notes explains
how to use the code for signal creation, alignment, analysis,
validation, and visualization described in Methods and Technical
Validation. Code Availability lists the code provided for
reproducibility and future research. We anticipate that this dataset and
analysis pipeline will support research on wire-cut evidence, including
future studies of real-world crime-scene evidence.

\subsection*{Cutting wires}\label{sec-cutting-wires}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Cutting wires}

We used 16-gauge, 1.5-mm anodized aluminum wire to create this wire-cut
dataset. For each cut, a 4-inch piece was unspooled and cut with a model
KWS-105 Kaiweets wire cutter at one of three blade locations (inner,
middle, or outer) to produce one replicate, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig-cut-tent-scan} (a). Each piece was then cut in half to
create 2-inch pieces for each pair of edges, AB and CD. A Sharpie line
marked the cut ends, yielding four samples. We then followed the
standard scanning protocol for the confocal microscope shown in
Figure~\ref{fig-cut-tent-scan} (b) to scan the wire-tip surfaces. The
surfaces were scanned at a resolution of
\(0.645 \mu m \times 0.645 \mu m\) per square pixel and saved in
\texttt{x3p} format. Figure~\ref{fig-cut-tent-scan} (c) shows the AB and
CD sides, with C on the reverse of A and D on the reverse of B. We
separated the AB and CD sides into two pieces along the bend, resulting
in eight scans. We repeated this process for all three blade locations
and five wire cutters, with two replicates for each tool-edge-location
combination, resulting in a sample of 120 scans. Each piece was labeled
according to the following naming convention: T(ool) 1/2/3/4/5, (Edge)
A/B/C/D, W(ire), L(ocation) I(nner)/M(iddle)/O(uter), and R(epetition)
1/2. For example, T1AW-LI-R1 denotes the first repetition of a piece cut
by tool 1 on edge A at the inner location. To minimize errors in the
sampling protocol, two team members cut and labeled the wires together.
One team member then scanned the samples in a specified order and named
the scans according to the naming convention, and a third team member
checked the saved data.

\subsection*{Extract profiles}\label{sec-extract-profiles}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Extract profiles}

Numerical comparisons between two replicates cannot be performed
directly on the \texttt{x3p} files. We must first extract representative
profiles from the scans. A profile is a sequence of surface-height
values along a user-drawn line. After processing, this profile becomes a
signal representing one scan and is used for subsequent comparisons. The
profile captures most features of the scan and is drawn orthogonally to
the striation marks, which are characterized by variations in groove
height. We therefore draw a line across the scan region to capture as
much striation information as possible, as shown in dark blue in
Figure~\ref{fig-T1AW-LI-R1-profiles-signals} (a). We then examine the
height values along this line. The resulting profile function is plotted
in Figure~\ref{fig-T1AW-LI-R1-profiles-signals} (b) and must be
processed before it becomes a useful signal.

\subsection*{Filtered signals}\label{sec-filtered-signals}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Filtered signals}

After extracting the profile, we process it to obtain the signal. Two
Gaussian filters, as discussed by Cleveland et al.
\citep{clevelandLocalRegressionModels1992}, are applied to each profile.
First, we use a large low-pass filter with a bandwidth of 400 microns to
remove the large-scale trend, which would otherwise overwhelm the
signal. We then use a small high-pass filter with a bandwidth of 40
microns to average noise and remove spikes. Finally, we remove the
extreme tail values and use the resulting signal for subsequent
comparisons, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig-T1AW-LI-R1-profiles-signals}
(c).

\subsection*{Align signals}\label{sec-align-signals}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Align signals}

After extracting signals from different scans, we compare them by
maximizing the CCF between each pair to determine their optimal
numerical alignment. We compare all possible pairs; for example, the
comparison between T1AW-LI-R1 and T1AW-LI-R2 corresponds to a row in
Figure~\ref{fig-scans-pair}. We expect signals from two replicates with
the same tool-edge-location combination, as shown in the first two
columns of Figure~\ref{fig-signals-pair-alignment}, to be relatively
similar. Their alignments should therefore show substantial overlap and
yield high CCF values close to 1. The alignments and values in the
rightmost column of Figure~\ref{fig-signals-pair-alignment} meet these
expectations.

\section*{Technical Validation}\label{sec-technical-validation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Technical Validation}

We conducted technical validation in two areas: (1) signal processing
and alignment and (2) variability assessment.

We validated scan processing and signal alignment as follows. We
expected high CCF values between signals from the same source and low
values between signals from different sources.
Figure~\ref{fig-signals-pair-alignment}, shown earlier in Align signals,
meets our expectation for same-source signals. As an example of a
different-source comparison, we selected T1AW-LI-R1 and T2AW-LI-R1,
shown in Figure~\ref{fig-T1AW-LI-R1} and Figure~\ref{fig-T2AW-LI-R1}.
Their signal alignment, shown in Figure~\ref{fig-T1AW-LI-R1-T2AW-LI-R1},
has a CCF value of 0.2, which is as low as expected. We also summarized
the resulting CCFs for all pairwise comparisons in a boxplot and a
receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig-ccf-boxplot} and Figure~\ref{fig-ccf-ROC}. The ROC curve
plots sensitivity, or the true positive rate, against the false positive
rate (FPR; 1 - specificity). The curve is close to the upper-left
corner, indicating strong classification performance. A threshold of
0.589 controls the FPR below 0.05 with a false negative rate (FNR) of 0,
whereas a threshold of 0.658 controls the FPR below 0.01 with an FNR of
0.02.

The second validation assessed variability in the pipeline across
replicates, stagings, and acquisitions. We selected scans from tool 2 at
the middle location for this assessment. Replicate variability was
assessed using two different cuts at the same edge and position, as
illustrated by the comparison in Figure~\ref{fig-signals-pair-alignment}
in Align signals. To assess staging variability, we rescanned replicate
2 under the confocal microscope and introduced the staging label S into
the naming protocol. The S1 designation is omitted from labels, so R2 is
equivalent to R2-S1. For all four edges of tool 2 at the middle
location, we created three stagings (R2, R2-S2, and R2-S3), computed
pairwise CCFs between stagings, and calculated their mean and standard
deviation (SD).

Within each staging, we further examined the effect of different
acquisitions by keeping the samples on the confocal microscope while
varying the lighting conditions. We introduced A as the acquisition
label. For edge A at staging 3, we performed four acquisitions and
omitted the A1 designation from the first acquisition label, so R2-S3 is
equivalent to R2-S3-A1. For the other three edges, we conducted two
acquisitions at staging 3, denoted by R2-S3 and R2-S3-A2. We again
computed pairwise CCFs between stagings and calculated their mean and
standard deviation.

With this setup, we expected CCF values to become more consistent from
replicate to staging to acquisition as the scanning conditions became
progressively more stable. Consequently, variability should decrease
along this sequence. We also compared replicates, stagings, and

stagings than between stagings and replicates. We visualized the results
as scatter plots in Figure~\ref{fig-rep_staging_acquisition}, which also

decrease in the standard deviation. The detailed numerical results,

We conducted pairwise comparisons and visualized some of the results in
Align signals and Technical Validation. Here, we present additional
analysis plots. When the CCF values are displayed in a tile map across
tools, locations, and edge combinations, we expect only the diagonal to
contain high values close to 1, shown in orange, because the diagonal
represents same-source comparisons. We expect the rest of the matrix to
contain low values close to 0, shown in gray. The pattern in
Figure~\ref{fig-ccf-tilemap} is generally consistent with this
expectation, except for a few cases involving edge D of tool 5. The
density plot in Figure~\ref{fig-ccf-density} shows the distributions of
CCF values for same-source and different-source comparisons. The overlap
between the tails of these distributions provides a rough threshold for
drawing conclusions.
